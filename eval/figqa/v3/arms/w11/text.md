# A feature selection-based oblique hyperplane for oblique random survival forests

#### Abstract

**Background** Recently, several machine learning (ML) algorithms for right-censored data, including Oblique Random Survival Forest (ORSF), have been utilized to develop risk prediction tools in cardiovascular disease (CVD) and oncology research. ORSF employs hyperplanes to represent a singular split, as opposed to the conventional univariate (axis) based approach, such as Random Survival Forest (RSF). However, ORSF encounters a hurdle in identifying the relevant features while constructing the hyperplane. Hence, we aim to propose and evaluate the predictive performance of three novel feature selection-based hyperplanes for ORSF. **Methods** We propose three variants of ORSF: (a) ORSF-LASSO, based on LASSO regression, (b) ORSF-MRMR, based on the Minimum Redundancy Maximum Relevance (MRMR) framework, and (c) ORSF-CARS, based on correlation- adjusted regression survival (CARS) score. Nine versions of these variants were evaluated against the Penalized Cox Proportional Hazards Model, RSF, and the original ORSF on ten public CVD and oncology datasets using Harrell’s C-index, D-Calibration, and integrated Brier score (IBS). The models were trained using three-fold cross-validation in R version 4.2.1 and the mlr3 ecosystem. **Results** The newly proposed models have shown high discrimination in CVD datasets, with ORSF-LASSO-min being the most consistently best-performing model. Furthermore, in CVD datasets, one of the proposed models demonstrated the lowest D-calibration compared to existing models. In oncology datasets, one or more new models outperformed existing models in three out of five datasets. ORSF-MRMR-3q, a novel model, exhibited the lowest D-calibration across two oncology datasets. The sensitivity analysis indicated that the performance of the newly proposed methods aligned with the primary analysis. **Conclusion** Our findings suggest that the three proposed new variants have the potential to predict time-to-event outcomes in CVD and oncology prognosis research. Nonetheless, the proposed methods need to be validated in comprehensive and varied datasets with prolonged follow-up periods and across multiple health domains. **Keywords** Time-to-event data, Oblique Random Survival Forest, LASSO Cox Proportional Hazards Model, Minimum Redundancy Maximum Relevance (MRMR) algorithm, Correlation-Adjusted Regression Survival (CARS) score, Cardiovascular Disease, Oncology

#### Introduction

Artificial Intelligence (AI), encompassing machine learn- ing (ML), has been in existence since the mid-1950s. The perceptron, proposed by Frank Rosenblatt in 1957, was the earliest design of artificial neural networks, laying the foundation for supervised ML models. Remarkably, it took over 30 years to implement the initial application of AI to time-to-event (TTE) analysis, commonly referred to as survival analysis, which estimates the duration until an outcome occurs, utilizing the tree-structured recursive partitioning proposed by Gordon and Olshen in 1985 [1]. Notably, in cardiovascular disease (CVD) research, the leading cause of death worldwide [2], the majority of AI algorithms were formulated within the domains of classification and regression [3, 4]. Classifica- tion and regression algorithms are known to induce bias and mis-calibrated predictions when applied in the con- text of censoring, a fundamental aspect of TTE data [5, 6]. In recent years, various AI models for right-censored data have been increasingly used for disease risk predic- tion tools such as Survival Trees [1], Random Survival Forest (RSF) [7], Support Vector Regression for cen- sored data [8], Neural network model for survival data [9], Non-linear Cox proportional hazard (Cox PH) model (DeepSurv) [10], Deep Learning Approach to Survival Analysis with Competing Risks (DeepHit) [11], and Neu- ral Multi-Task Logistic Regression (N-MTLR) [12]. Teshale et al. (2024) explored the application of ML and DL models for predicting survival outcome in CVD [13]. They conclude that RSF and DeepSurv models are currently the prevalent models for predicting CVD out- comes, with RSF marginally outperforming DeepSurv. Moreover, Sarica et al. performed the first comprehen- sive study addressing RSF explainability [14]. They pro- vide a comprehensive overview of the explainability of RSF in predicting the conversion-to-Alzheimer’s disease risk within 4 years. They reported stability and robust- ness of RSF. In 2017, Malgorzata Kretowska proposed an evolution- ary algorithm in survival data analysis [15]. She intro- duced the oblique survival tree, which uses hyperplanes to represent a single split instead of the usual univariate (axis) based survival trees, such as recursive partitioning for survival trees [16]. The Kaplan-Meier method is used to estimate the survival functions at the terminal nodes. The Oblique Random Survival Forest (ORSF) is an ensemble method for right-censored survival data first proposed by Janger et al. in 2019 [17, 18]. The difference between ORSF and RSF is that ORSF uses linear combi- nations of input variables to recursively partition a set of training data. The ORSF fits Cox PH or similar models into the non-terminal nodes of its survival trees. Linear combinations of input variables (LCIVs) are synthesized

using the coefficients estimated from the Cox PH models and used as splitting variables for non-leave nodes in the O R SF. The existing implementation of ORSF using the *aorsf* R package [18] utilizes only three criteria (fast, Cox PH, Cox net) to construct LCIVs. In ORSF-fast, the default method, the LCIVs, is fitted using a single iteration of Newton-Raphson scoring on the Cox partial likelihood function. In ORSF-cox, the linear combinations of pre- dictors are derived from the coefficients obtained from fitting a Cox PH regression. The latest version employs penalized Cox PH regression [19] at each node, which can be beneficial for feature selection. However, the user must manually determine the number of predictors uti- lized in the LCIVs. Driven by the objective of improving the prediction performance of the ORSF algorithm and addressing the feature selection issues, our study has the following major contributions:

1. Expand one existing method and propose two novel variants of linear combinations of variables of ORSF. These methods can enhance the survival prediction performance while simultaneously preserving the explainability of model predictions.
2. Assess the performance of these three methods compared to the original ORSF, conventional statistical methods, and RSF using appropriate performance metrics on publicly available real-world datasets.
3. Evaluate the proposed methods in terms of computational efficiency, since the training of the model in the real world is as important as its performance.
The paper is structured as follows: Section 2 presents an overview of ORSF applications in health data and out- lines the current modifications to ORSF. Section 3 intro- duces the proposed three new methods and describes their implementations. Section 4 delineates the compo- nents of the benchmark experiment, including the fea- tures of public cardiovascular and oncology datasets, the model training procedure, and the performance metrics. Section 5 presents the study’s main findings. Section 6 discusses the results we have obtained, while Sect. 7 articulates the conclusion and provides directions for further research.

#### Related work

The ORSF was first introduced in 2019, and in 2023, an accelerated and interpretable implementation was released using the *aorsf* R package. Consequently, only a limited number of ML research studies have addressed censored data in TTE analysis using this approach.

Segar et al. were among the pioneers in employing ORSF on real-world datasets [20]. They developed ML- Based Race-Specific Models to predict the 10-year risk of heart failure using six candidate ML-based and tra- ditional Cox PH algorithms, including ORSF, gradient boosted trees, and several variants of the Cox PH model. Their findings showed that the ORSF risk model dem- onstrated the highest discriminatory performance, as measured by the C-index, in external validation cohorts among Black and White individuals. Wisotzkey et al. used data from a large, comprehensive registry of pediat- ric heart transplant data to assess whether ML algorithms could enhance risk prediction of 1-year allograft loss in pediatric heart transplant patients [21]. Among the mod- els evaluated, ORSF achieved the highest C-statistic and lowest mean integrated calibration index, indicating superior discrimination and calibration. Eisner and team developed an ML model to predict cognitive impairment or dementia in older adults. The ORSF revealed reason- able discriminatory performance in this context [22]. Yu et al. trained models to predict overall survival for non- small cell lung cancer patients [23]. They implemented several nonlinear ML models, including Cox PH, RSF, and ORSF. In this study, however, RSF outperformed ORSF and other methods in terms of predictive accuracy. The authors of ORSF have also developed two addi- tional implementations of ORSF in the realms of classi- fication and regression. Furthermore, ORSF incorporates variable selection techniques to enhance model inter- pretability and performance. Hu et al. constructed ML models including-Oblique Random Forests (ORF), Ran- dom Forests (RF), and gradient boosted decision trees (XGBoost)- to identify patients with elevated systolic blood pressure surpassing 160 mmHg among hyperten- sive or pre-hypertensive individuals [24]. #### Proposed methods

This section describes the methods adopted to address existing challenges in feature selection and hyperpa- rameter tuning in ORSF. It first explains the three new methodologies proposed, which serve as new variants for creating the LCIVs within the ORSF framework. The section concludes by detailing how these proposed approaches are systematically applied at each non-termi- nal node to compute the necessary coefficients for con- structing these LCIVs. As previously mentioned, the linear combinations of input variables (LCIVs) in the ORSF algorithm are derived from three methods: fast, Cox PH, and Net. The first and second methods construct LCIVs using both rel- evant and non-relevant variables. In the third method, a penalized regression is used to perform feature selection at non-terminal nodes. However, the number of non- zero coefficients (i.e., variables) that are returned from penalized regression should be specified in advance. Cer- tainly, this number could be adjusted during the hyper- parameter tuning phase. However, doing so would result in unnecessary computational overhead. To address the challenges of feature selection and hyperparameter tun- ing of the ORSF algorithm mentioned earlier, we pro- pose three new variants of the methodology to create the LCIVs, hereafter referred to as ORSF-LASSO, ORSF- MRMR, and ORSF-CARS. Figure 1 illustrates the deriva- tion of LCIVs at non-terminal nodes in the ORSF and the proposed ORSF algorithm.

**Minimum redundancy maximum relevance method** There are two major approaches to feature selection: wrapper methods and filter methods [28, 29]. Wrap- per methods employ a designated learning algorithm to assess multiple feature subsets, determining their use- fulness based on the algorithm’s performance with each subset. They frequently identify a compact, non-redun- dant feature collection, resulting in increased prediction accuracy due to their selection [29, 30]. However, they may incur significant computing expenses since they require searching through several feature subsets and training a learning algorithm for each subset [31].

Filter methods, on the other hand, are pre-processing steps where features are selected based on their unique characteristics, indicating their relevance or usefulness in distinguishing between different outcome categories. Importantly, they are fast, computationally efficient, and their feature selection criteria are independent of the learning process, facilitating improved generalization to unseen data [31]. A common filter method involves selecting top-ranked features based on a scoring metric, which may lead to redundancy by including correlated features and inefficiency by retaining unnecessary ones. To address this, Ding and Peng introduced the Minimum Redundancy Maximum Relevance (MRMR) framework as a filter method that explicitly endeavors to reduce redun- dancy while maintaining relevant characteristics [31]. The MRMR algorithm was first introduced in contexts with a large number of potential features, such as micro- array gene expression data, where identifying a small set of genes that accurately classify phenotypes is cru- cial [31]. The MRMR framework aims to select features that are not only maximally relevant to the target classes but also minimally redundant with each other. Hence, we need two metrics to quantify relevance and redun- dancy. In the case of categorical features, for example, the relevance of a feature *x₁* with respect to the outcome

*y* is measured using mutual information [32]. A higher mutual information indicates a stronger dependency between the feature and the outcome, which would result in a higher level of relevance. Redundancy between two features, *x₁* and *x₂*, is also measured using mutual infor- mation. However, the minimum redundancy condi- tion aims to minimize the average mutual information between all pairs of selected features. Finally, the MRMR framework combines the maximum relevance and mini- mum redundancy criteria into a single criterion function. MRMR has been effectively employed for feature selec- tion in several applications, including binary classifica- tion and time-to-event analysis. Spooner et al. evaluate eight feature selection approaches, including MRMR, for predicting survival time to dementia [33]. Leger et al. used MRMR and eleven feature selection approaches to predict overall survival in locally progressed head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) patients [34]. Toğaçar et al. utilized MRMR to select lung X-ray features for pneumonia diagnosis, resulting in robust, consistent features and reducing feature set dimension effectively [35]. Xing et al. developed a model combin- ing MRMR and artificial neural network (ANN) to pre- dict breast cancer patient survival [36]. Mohamed et al. utilized a metaheuristic approach and MRMR for feature selection in drug response microarray classification [37]. We determine the factors to use in the creation of LCIVs based on empirical quantiles. Variables with scores above the empirical quantiles, such as 25% (q1), 50% (q2), and 75% (q3) of the score distribution for all candidate variables, will be incorporated into the Cox PH model. The coefficients of these variables will then be uti- lized to generate the LCIVs. We compute the scores uti- lizing the *mRMRe: an R package for parallelized mRMR* *ensemble feature selection* [38] and designate the sug- gested new extensions as ORSF-mrmr-q1, ORSF-mrmr- q2, and ORSF-mrmr-q3, accordingly.

**Correlation-adjusted regression survival score method** Traditional Cox PH regression models struggle in high- dimensional survival analysis with too many potential genetic markers. Univariate screening, which assesses the association between each marker and survival sepa- rately, using Cox scores, overlooks marker correlations, leading to suboptimal selection, especially when mark- ers are highly correlated [39]. The initial application, in 2012, of the correlation-adjusted regression (CAR) score approach was in linear regression, as detailed in the work of Zuber et al. [40]. They demonstrated that estimated CAR scores resulted in improved variable rankings and

higher predictive performance compared to other vari- able selection and modeling techniques, such as lasso [27] or boosting [41]. Welchowski et al., in 2019, introduced the correlation- adjusted regression survival (CARS) score as an exten- sion of the CAR approach for right-censored data in cancer research, addressing the lack of such an exten- sion [39]. The CARS score, in contrast to Cox scores, first applies a decorrelating transformation to the mark- ers to remove their inter-correlations. It then quantifies the association between these decorrelated markers and the (log-transformed) survival time. This allows CARS to identify markers that have a unique contribution to sur- vival prediction, even in the presence of highly correlated markers, which Cox scores might redundantly select or miss due to their interdependencies. Welchowski and colleagues compared CARS scores, Cox scores, and *L₁*-penalized Cox PH regression [19] across various scenarios using simulation and real-world datasets on prostate and breast cancer [39]. Using simu- lation, CARS scores generally achieved higher precision- recall AUC values than Cox scores, especially in high marker correlation scenarios. In breast cancer micro- array data analysis, CARS scores showed better pre- dictive performance than Cox scores. Bommert et al. benchmark CARS against several filter methods using 11 high-dimensional gene expression survival data sets and conclude that CARS performs best with respect to the integrated Brier score on all considered data sets [42]. Khaniki et al. use CARS to screen the genes in identifying new biomarkers in colorectal cancer [43, 44]. Based on false non-discovery rates and empirical quan- tiles, we identify significant variables for inclusion in LCIVs. The Cox PH model will be used to calculate the coefficients to be used in LCIVs for variables identified with false non- discovery rates or their scores above the empirical quan- tiles, which are 25% (q1), 50% (q2), and 75% (q3) of the score distribution for all candidate variables. We employ the *Correlation-Adjusted Regression Survival Scores* R package [45] to calculate the scores and designate the pro- posed new extensions as ORSF-CARS-fndr, ORSF-CARS- q1, ORSF-CARS-q2, and ORSF-CARS-q3, respectively.

The algorithm’s operation can be detailed as fol- lows: The algorithm processes training data consisting of features (Dx) for multiple samples and correspond- ing survival times with event indicators (Dy). It starts by defining configuration values for various model fitting techniques, such as LASSO, MRMR, and CARS score, to compute weight coefficients (β) for the selected input variables, which are later utilized to create LCIVs. Initially, β is set to zero, and the algorithm operates on non-leaf nodes through a decision-making process. A suf- ficient sample size of at least 15 is required to proceed with coefficient determination. If LASSO is selected, a LASSO COX PH model is employed with cross-validation, followed by the β coefficients assigned from the model’s coefficients. For MRMR, a subset of variables is selected using its specific criteria before fitting a COX PH model to derive coefficients. The CARS score method similarly identifies variables based on established criteria and fits a COX PH model for coefficient assignment. When the sample size is inadequate, coefficients are randomly deter- mined. In case of errors during the coefficient determina- tion methods, β defaults to a random uniform distribution.

**Methods to be compared** We evaluated nine criteria in this experiment, includ- ing two criteria from ORSF-LASSO (ORSF-LASSO-min and ORSF-LASSO-se), three criteria from ORSF-MRMR (ORSF-MRMR-q1, ORSF-MRMR-q2, and ORSF- MRMR-q3), and four criteria from ORSF-CARS (ORSF- CARS-fndr, ORSF-CARS-q1, ORSF-CARS-q2, and ORSF-CARS-q3). These nine criteria derived from the three new variants are compared to Penalized Cox PH Model (Penalized Cox PH), RSF with gradient-based (global non-quantile) Brier score splitting as splitting rule, and ORSF with three cri- teria (fast, Cox PH, Cox net) from ORSF with C-index as splitting rule.

**Models training and performance evaluation** In data preprocessing, we scaled continuous variables and applied treatment (dummy) coding to categori- cal variables, while missing values were imputed using Recursive Partitioning and Regression Trees. Initially, each dataset was randomly divided using a fixed seed into threefold cross-validation, stratified by the event to

preserve the original distribution of occurrences, par- ticularly in instances of significant censoring (e.g., 90%). Three-fold outer resampling was used for the perfor- mance evaluation of the algorithms. Inner resampling for hyperparameters tuning was carried out using hold-out (70%/30%). In the inner resampling, the optimal values of hyperparameters that maximized performance on the training set were identified by 100 iterations of random- ized search inside 70% of the training set. The complete list of hyperparameters for the examined techniques in this benchmark experiment is provided in Table S1. The hyperparameters for RSF and ORSF com- prised the number of trees, the number of features to consider for optimal splitting in RSF and for construct- ing the linear combination in ORSF, and the minimum number of samples necessary at a leaf node. The split- ting criteria in RSF and ORSF are based on the gradient- based (global non-quantile) Brier score and the C-index, respectively. Penalized Cox PH model hyperparameters included *L1* and *L2* regularization. Models’ discrimination was assessed using Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) [59], while we assessed calibration using the D-Calibration measure [60] and integrated Brier score (IBS) [61]. A higher C-index sug- gests that the model has strong discrimination power, while lower D-calibration and IBS values indicate that the model is well-calibrated.

**Sensitivity analysis** We generated four synthetic datasets from the rotter- dam and TRACE datasets to examine the efficacy of the suggested approaches in the context of missing values and imbalanced datasets. For imbalanced datasets, we employed simple random under-sampling to randomly eliminate samples from the positive class in rotterdam and TRACE datasets. The new datasets, rotterdam_ reduced and TRACE_reduced, have sample sizes of 1631 and 1150, with incidence prevalence of 10% and 20%, respectively. We incorporated 20% missing completely at random (MCAR) values in the two datasets, utilizing the methods specified for generating multivariate missing data in the *mice* package in R [62]. The datasets are des- ignated as rotterdam_missing and TRACE_missing, with missing values imputed utilizing the same methods as in the primary study.

#### Discussion

We presented a feature selection-based framework employing embedded (LASSO) and filter (MRMR and CARS) feature selection strategies to construct the oblique hyperplane at each non-terminal node of ORSF. We evaluated the discriminative and calibration efficacy of the proposed models in comparison to baseline mod- els utilizing ten clinical datasets from CVD and oncology. In our first approach, LASSO, we propose replac- ing three methods: fast, Cox PH, and Net with LASSO Cox PH, which uses three-fold cross-validation to iden- tify *λminandλSE*. In terms of discriminative perfor- mance, our proposed ORSF-LASSO-min model attained a superior C-Index compared to baseline models in the GRACE and SMARTO datasets. Our proposed models outperformed the transformer survival prediction mod- els suggested by Kaushal et al. in terms of C-index for the GRACE dataset [68]. Furthermore, we observed that the existing ML approaches showed comparable perfor- mance to the Penalized Cox PH model, as the inclusion

of irrelevant features is inherent in all these techniques, compounded by the limited number of features and the small sample size of the datasets. One version (1q) of the second proposed model, ORSF-MRMR, exhibited a superior C-Index com- pared to the baseline and proposed models in GRACE, but ORSF-MRMR-3q demonstrated markedly inferior D-Calibration performance in the SMARTO dataset. Comparable results were noted in benchmark tests per- formed by Leger et al. and Xing et al. [34, 36]. These find- ings, however, contradict the conclusions of Spooner et al., likely due to the disparity in disease (dementia versus CVD) and the number of features analyzed (251 and 185 compared to 5) [33]. The third proposed model, ORSF-CARS, exhibits a superior C-Index compared to the baseline models in the GRACE dataset and demonstrates equivalent per- formance in the COST and SMARTO datasets. Regard- ing calibration, ORSF-CARS exhibited comparable IBS to baseline models (Penalized Cox PH and ORSF-cph) and dramatically reduced IBS compared to ORSF-MRMR versions. This outcome aligns with a prior comparative analysis conducted by Bommert et al., which utilized gene expression data [42]. The computational time demands for the proposed algorithms escalate significantly with the dataset’s dimen- sionality. This aligns with the notion that larger datasets necessitate increased processing resources and time for

model training. The extension of training duration poses significant challenges. However, it is critical to recognize that the methodologies and procedures are implemented in R, whereas ORSF is developed in C++. The inter-call- ing of functions between R and C + + incurs significant computational costs. In our forthcoming endeavors, we intend to re-write these approaches in C + + and incorpo- rate them directly into the source code of the ORSF pack- age to minimize training duration. The main limitation of our work is the size of the data- set employed for model training. The proposed methods may demonstrate a more pronounced effect when utilized on a larger dataset with prolonged follow-up periods. Secondly, in this benchmarking, we employed data from CVD and oncology studies. We intend to evaluate the models in other health domains within a more heteroge- neous patient population to confirm their generalizability. The selections of CARS and MRMR versions (i.e., the fea- ture selection proportions) were made arbitrarily due to the ORSF package’s custom hyperplane, which required hardcoding the values (1q, 2q, etc.), thereby preventing any modification of these parameters during hyperpa- rameter optimization. Furthermore, due to the limited computational power of the HPC cluster, we were unable to investigate the performance of the suggested models across different cut-off values for CARS and MRMR. The methodological significance of our study lies in pro- viding a feature selection-based framework that systemati- cally and automatically constructs the oblique hyperplane in ORSF. It addresses inherent feature selection challenges in existing ORSF implementations. It efficiently identifies relevant features while constructing these hyperplanes and explicitly selects or weights significant variables, hence enhancing time-to-event models’ interpretability. This study lays a foundation for future investigations into vari- ous feature selection techniques. The authors streamline this process by a recent contribution to *mlr3extralearners* package version 1.1.0 [69]. This contribution will facilitate benchmarking different feature selection algorithms in a “plug-and-play” manner using the *mlr3* ecosystem.

#### Conclusion and future research

The present study shows that integrating the proposed feature selection-based framework improves the cali- bration of predicting time-to-event across various CVD and oncology datasets. In particular, the ORSF-LASSO- min and ORSF-CARS variants proved to be promising approaches. We validated that the suggested extensions have discrimination capability in predicting an individu- al’s risk, as well as other ML and statistical models. We then demonstrated that specific versions of the proposed methods had comparable training time to the original ORSF without tuning the number of features required to construct the hyperplane. We also released an R script

for the implementation of the three proposed methods and an R6 class for *aorsf* learner to facilitate running benchmarking experiments of the proposed methods in mlr3 ecosystem, see [67] for more details. In future work, we aim to include native C + + implemen- tations for the proposed methods, since the computational cost of certain methods highlights the need for further optimization. Furthermore, we should focus on validating these methods in larger, more diverse datasets or through a dedicated simulation study. Explore automated tuning of variable selection criterion (i.e., try different thresholds) in MRMR and CARS algorithms to maximize their potential. Finally, in our subsequent study, we are interested in eval- uating the efficacy of other feature selection algorithms, including Relief-based algorithms [70].
